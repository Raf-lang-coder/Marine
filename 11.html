Ocean Ecosystem Overview

The ocean covers over 70% of the Earth's surface and is home to millions of marine species. Coral reefs, deep-sea trenches, and seagrass beds play vital roles in regulating global temperatures and maintaining atmospheric balance.

Marine biodiversity is essential to ecological stability. Coastal ecosystems provide crucial nursery grounds for sea turtles, fishes, and benthic organisms, serving as a buffer against coastal erosion and habitat loss.

Key Ocean Habitats

  • Coral Reefs: Vibrant biodiversity hubs supporting nearly 25% of all ocean life.
  • Seagrass Beds: Underwater meadows providing vital forage for marine species like the Pawikan.
  • Pelagic Zones: Open ocean expanses where migratory sea life travels thousands of miles.

Pawikan (Sea Turtle) Overview

Pawikan in English: sea turtle | Meaning

Detailed Meaning / Definition

  • Pawikan is the Filipino/Tagalog word for sea turtle, a marine reptile with flippers and a hard or leathery shell.
  • The term appears across several Philippine languages (Tagalog, Cebuano, Bikol, Pangasinan), all referring to sea turtles.
  • Sea turtles are protected under Philippine law due to their endangered status and ecological importance.

The pawikan is a large sea turtle native to the Philippines, known for its hard shell, long migrations, and critical role in marine ecosystems.

Biological Characteristics

Pawikan are large marine reptiles with flippers instead of feet, allowing them to swim efficiently in the ocean. Their carapace (shell) measures about 90 to 120 centimeters in length and can be olive, reddish-brown, or yellowish in color. They have a single pair of lungs and must surface to breathe, and their body temperature varies with the surrounding water. Unlike land turtles, pawikan cannot retract their head and limbs into their shell. They feed primarily on seagrass and other marine vegetation, which contributes to the health of coral reefs and seagrass beds.

Species and Distribution

In the Philippines, five species of pawikan are considered endangered: Chelonia mydas (green turtle), Dermochelys coriacea (leatherback), Eretmochelys imbricata (hawksbill), Lepidochelys olivacea (olive ridley), and Caretta caretta (loggerhead). They inhabit tropical and subtropical waters worldwide, except polar regions, and can travel thousands of miles across oceans. Female pawikan return to the beaches where they were born to lay eggs, often after 25-30 years.

Facts About Pawikan

  • Native Reptile: Pawikan are native reptiles found in coastal areas of San Jose, especially in Brgy. Funda-Dalipe, Mojon, Malaiba, and Barangay 4.
  • Nesting Season: Pawikan nest on sandy beaches between August and December, with hatchlings mostly seen from October to November.
  • Conservation Efforts: Local conservation efforts include rescue, rehabilitation, and release programs at the Pawikan Center in Funda-Dalipe, where community events and coastal cleanups help protect their habitats.
  • Threats: Threats to pawikan include habitat destruction, poaching, pollution, climate change, and accidental capture in fishing gear.
  • Community Involvement: Collaborative efforts by local officials, environmental agencies, and volunteers work to safeguard these endangered turtles and raise public awareness through education and cleanup initiatives.
